## Background
Foundation models are rapidly becoming distributed intelligence systems. In deep learning, we use distributed optimization to train a neural network over physically or virtually distributed computational workers to improve training speed, performance, and sometimes privacy preservation. As a result, we can apply distributed optimization to train a base LLM. On the other hand, with a trained LLM serving as the basis for an agent, multi-agent reasoning explores how we can let multiple agents collaborate with each other only at the inference level to effectively resolve questions. We can see that, for real-world products, we are beginning to explore the opportunities arising from distributed optimization and multi-agent collaboration.

Therefore, a natural question is: what is the gap between developing multi-agent reasoning and distributed optimization? Why do we need to adapt from distributed optimization to multi-agent reasoning, and how can we heuristically apply the ideas from distributed optimization to multi-agent reasoning?

### Distributed Optimization
Over the past decade, we have shifted from conventional training and inference of neural networks on a single machine to distributed training and inference as the scale of models and data continues to grow. We let multiple computational workers collaboratively optimize objectives through local computation, communication, and aggregation. Today, distributed optimization is a part of the infrastructure used to train a successful LLM.

In general, we can have a canonical formulation where our objective is to 

$$
\min_{\theta} F(\theta)=\min_{\theta} p_i f_i(\theta)
$$

where $\theta$ is the neural network we want to optimize in a distributed manner. We use $f_i(\theta)$ to represent the local objective on each computational worker $i$. Then, we can have different forms of $p_i$: they can represent the importance of each local objective (e.g., when finding Pareto-optimal solutions), or we can define them as $p_i=\frac{\lvert d_i \rvert}{\sum_j \lvert d_j \rvert}$, meaning the proportion of samples ($d_i$) on each device. How $p_i$ and $f_i$ are determined depends on the actual task we want to complete.

Distributed optimization can have different settings. Here we provide some classic examples. For example, distributed training is one of the most common ways to train a neural network, such as a foundation model. Apart from training models in data centers, people have also developed gossip-based P2P decentralized training for geographically distributed LLM pretraining. Federated learning is another form of distributed optimization in which data are kept locally.

## Multi-Agent Reasoning
LLM-powered agents are increasingly deployed for complex, multi-step tasks. Recently, people have begun to explore how we can let multiple agents collaborate with each other to solve one complex task or multiple different tasks at the same time. In multi-agent collaboration, the canonical paradigm is at the inference level, where text is the main communication modality. Agents exchange inference outputs with each other to complete one task together or collaborate with each other.

There are different applications of multi-agent reasoning. One common application is that multiple agents collaborate with each other to resolve one complex problem through different communication protocols and collaboration strategies, such as [proving the Riemann Hypothesis](https://alpo.ge/remark.pdf), [rigidifying a floppy molecule](https://deepmind.google/blog/agentic-cheminformatics-with-google-antigravity/), and [solving the Navier–Stokes Millennium Prize Problem](https://openai.com/index/navier-stokes-solution/). Also, similar to federated learning and distributed training, multi-agent systems can work on different tasks in their own domains and communicate with each other to share insights and improve each individual agent, as in Federation over Text <d-cite key="yao2026federation"></d-cite>, federated agent optimization <d-cite key="yang2026federated"></d-cite>, and Dynamic Agent Skills <d-cite key="li2026dynamic"></d-cite>. Multiple agents can also interact in a decentralized way, like a research town <d-cite key="liu2023dynamic,chen2024agentverse,yu2024researchtown"></d-cite>. In summary, in multi-agent settings, we can use agents based on the same LLM or different LLMs to solve a single task or their respective individual tasks.

Nevertheless, although we can use a general form $\max_{\mathcal{A}}\mathcal{R}(\mathcal{A})$ to denote the objective of maximizing rewards with agents, there is no clear optimization formulation we can directly work with. Hence, when we encounter various [challenges](#shared-common-challenges) in multi-agent collaboration, it is harder for us to optimize as we do in distributed optimization. As a result, in this post, we are going to try to draw a connection between distributed optimization and multi-agent reasoning so that, when we deal with multi-agent reasoning, we can apply optimization ideas to new agent scenarios and make the problems easier to resolve.

# Shared Common Challenges
We next describe three of the core shared challenges associated with distributed optimization and multi-agent reasoning. From these challenges, we can conceptually see how ideas for solving challenges in distributed optimization can inform solutions to challenges in multi-agent reasoning.

## Performance
The first challenge is how we can improve performance, which is accuracy in most cases. If we give a distributed system the same amount of data, the challenge is how we can help downstream users complete their tasks as well as possible.

<link rel="stylesheet" href="{{ '/assets/html/2027-11-23-do2ma/challenges-table.css' | relative_url }}">

| Challenges | Distributed Optimization | Multi-Agent Collaboration |
|:-----------|:-------------------------|:----------------------|
|Aggregation| How can we aggregate model weights or gradients distributed over devices?|How can we coordinate agents' reasoning outputs to produce a good final answer?|
|Data Distribution|Distributed workers can hold different numbers of samples, or their data may not be independently and identically distributed. | Distributed agents can hold different numbers of tasks, and these tasks can come from different domains and be of different types (coding, question answering, or computer use agent tasks).|
|Personalization|Different clients may want to hold different local models rather than a global model and achieve different objectives|Different agents may want to hold a different memory and harness and resolve different types of tasks.|
{: .do2ma-challenges}

## Efficiency
Along with performance, there are many challenges that limit efficiency in both scenarios. These concern not only statistical efficiency, such as convergence, but also system efficiency, such as the wall-clock time needed for training or inference.

| Challenges | Distributed Optimization | Multi-Agent Collaboration |
|:-----------|:-------------------------|:----------------------|
| Statistical Heterogeneity| How do we derive a guaranteed theoretical bound for convergence? |How do we decide how many rounds or iterations multiple agents need to communicate with each other before stopping?|
| Systems Heterogeneity| Devices can vary in hardware (CPU, GPU, memory) and network connectivity (NVLink, PCIe, Wi-Fi).| Different agents can have different hardware, network connectivity, and token budgets.|
|Expensive Communication| Frequent exchange of model weights or gradients between workers will cause a heavy communication load.| Excessive exchange of long model reasoning outputs and chains of thought will cause a heavy communication load.|
{: .do2ma-challenges}

## Trustworthiness
The third important challenge in a common distributed system would be trustworthiness: how to ensure that the results distributed systems generate are reliable and trustworthy. More importantly, it has been a long-term challenge to preserve the privacy of data on distributed workers and ensure that the whole distributed system is sufficiently robust, safe, and secure against various vulnerabilities and potential malicious attacks.

| Challenges | Distributed Optimization | Multi-Agent Collaboration |
|:-----------|:-------------------------|:----------------------|
|Robustness| How do we guarantee correctness if some workers are malicious and submit suboptimal model weights and gradients? |How do we guarantee the correctness of skills and reasoning if some workers are malicious and submit faulty chains of thought or reasoning trajectories?|
|Safety and Security|How do we handle the case where one client acts alone or several colluding clients work together to break and corrupt the system?|How do we deal with the vulnerability where one agent acts alone or several colluding agents work together to exploit and break the whole system?|
|Privacy| How do we preserve the sensitive information in each worker's local data? |How do we make sure that the sensitive information in the prompt, task description, and task environment will not be exposed to other agents?|
{: .do2ma-challenges}

In summary, we can see that distributed optimization and multi-agent collaboration share important challenges, with similar high-level ideas underlying the two schemes.

# Exploring Multi-Agent Collaboration through the Lens of Distributed Optimization

In this part, we would like to answer the core question of what benefits we can get if we bridge distributed optimization and multi-agent collaboration, and how this will help us resolve the challenges in the new multi-agent collaboration community. First, we would like to give a canonical formulation through which we can view Federation over Text<d-cite key="yao2026federationharness"></d-cite> and Federation over Harness in terms of FedAvg<d-cite key="mcmahan2017communication"></d-cite>.

Federation over Text is a federated-like framework that enables a set of agents to collaboratively and iteratively build an evolving library of reusable insights from independent local reasoning traces. We can formalize the utility of an insight library $l$ as $U(l)$, which is an average of the utilities across all agents it serves: $U(l) = \frac{1}{N} \sum_{i=1}^{N} U(A(x_i, l))$. $U(A(x_i, l))$ denotes the utility of agent $i$ solving its task $x_i$ using some base LLM with reference to the external library $l$. In FoT, each agent independently solves some task $x_i$ with a base language model, and then the thinking processes of multiple agents solving different but related tasks are aggregated together. Finally, an insight library $l$ is produced, which is a high-level aggregation of the common principles and ideas shared across the agents’ tasks.

We can see that FoT is analogous to classic federated/distributed learning, where these two regimes can be interpreted within the same conceptual framework. This mapping allows us to transfer concrete algorithmic principles from distributed optimization (such as staleness weighting, robust aggregation, and personalization) to the design of semantic aggregation rules, as discussed in detail later in this thrust.

We can see that both share a workflow in each iteration of local computation, keeping raw data local and uploading processed content (reasoning traces of reflection and updated model weights instead of raw task completion trajectories and raw data). Then, a server aggregates the uploaded content into reusable content (an insight library or harness and global model weights) so that existing distributed workers and agents can use it to improve performance. Also, when new workers and agents are added in the future, they can use it directly to complete tasks. The idea in FedAvg helps FoT/FoH adopt a similar design in a federated setting to resolve the research question of how to aggregate multiple agents' reasoning while keeping the raw trajectories local.

## Performance
{: #performance-research}

In conventional distributed optimization, when we aim to improve performance, we focus on developing theoretical analyses or empirical methods to optimize a well-defined formulation. In multi-agent reasoning, one important challenge arising from the lack of a clear formulation is that we do not have clearly defined components as in distributed optimization. Hence, a natural idea is to first consider existing approaches in distributed optimization and then map each component to its counterpart in multi-agent collaboration. Since many of these designs are supported by rigorous theoretical analysis or extensive empirical evidence in distributed optimization, we can leverage similar conceptual analogies to experimentally or theoretically investigate whether they are also effective in multi-agent collaboration.

For example, in distributed optimization, a common approach is to use AllReduce or federated learning algorithms to optimize the process of collaborative model training across distributed devices. In multi-agent collaboration, we can adopt similar communication topologies to structure interactions between agents. For instance, we can follow the communication pattern of AllReduce to allow agents to communicate and exchange information with each other.

Another example is personalization, a classical problem in distributed optimization. We can explicitly define local and global models and employ optimization methods such as Ditto<d-cite key="li2021ditto"></d-cite> to achieve personalization. In multi-agent collaboration, when directly addressing personalization, it is unclear which components of each agent should be optimized, such as its prompt or harness design. Similarly, by mapping local and global models, as well as the mechanisms for aggregating gradients or model updates, to local and global agents and their mechanisms for sharing reasoning traces, we can approach the personalization problem in a more systematic manner.

| Challenges | Research Question Studied in Multi-Agent Collaboration | Ideas from Distributed Optimization |
|:-----------|:------------------------------------------------|:------------------------------------|
| Aggregation | Combine diverse reasoning and reflections from multiple agentic systems into a reusable knowledge base or a good final answer through a well-designed topology and agent deployment structure, such as multi-agent debate <d-cite key="du2024debate"></d-cite>, a federated setting <d-cite key="yao2026federation"></d-cite>, and a specific communication topology <d-cite key="zhou2026mass"></d-cite>. | Adapt distributed communication and model-design strategies such as AllReduce <d-cite key="patarasuk2009allreduce"></d-cite>, FedAvg <d-cite key="mcmahan2017communication"></d-cite>, and federated neural architecture search <d-cite key="yao2024perfedrlnas"></d-cite>.|
| Data Distribution | Effectively distill knowledge from agents with uneven task coverage, different domains, and asymmetric local information to share useful knowledge <d-cite key="yao2026federationharness,mohtashami2023social"></d-cite> and coordinate agents with asymmetric information <d-cite key="liu2024asymmetry"></d-cite>. | Use algorithms to cluster clients with related objectives and group clients to reduce interference between unrelated domains <d-cite key="ghosh2020clustered"></d-cite>. |
| Personalization | Each agent retains user-specific memory and behavior while benefiting from shared experience, as in MAEL <d-cite key="li2025experiential"></d-cite> and LEGOMem<d-cite key="han2025legomem"></d-cite>.| Use Ditto's idea of separating global and personalized models <d-cite key="li2021ditto"></d-cite> to maintain a shared global model together with personalized client models. |
{: .do2ma-challenges .do2ma-performance-ideas}

## Efficiency
{: #efficiency-research}
At the efficiency level, people may think that multi-agent collaboration will differ more from distributed optimization. For example, in multi-agent collaboration at the text inference level, efficiency may be related to the number of tokens. Agents and LLMs may tend to generate long responses, and the length is not deterministic. In distributed optimization, however, the updated parts are model parameters, which are usually of a fixed size.

Nevertheless, similar ideas and techniques can still be transferred. For example, to tackle system heterogeneity, a series of techniques in distributed optimization have been proposed to deal with issues such as differences in device memory and connections, where some GPUs may communicate via NVLink while some can only communicate via PCIe. Similarly, among multiple agents, it is not guaranteed that the systems have the same resources to run agents: some may have faster systems, and some may have larger token budgets. At the same time, some agents can have fast network connections, such as 5G or connected networks, while some may only have slow LTE connections. Similar structures have been studied in distributed optimization and can be adapted to the new multi-agent challenges. Another instance is communication, where we can adapt the original pruning and communication optimization techniques from the gradient and parameter level to the text sent between agents.

| Challenges | Research Question Studied in Multi-Agent Collaboration | Ideas from Distributed Optimization |
|:-----------|:------------------------------------------------|:------------------------------------|
| Statistical Heterogeneity | Stop communication early to reduce communication when the reasoning tends to converge<d-cite key="hu2025stability,niu2026armormad,yao2026federationharness"></d-cite>. | Reduce communication and stop early according to local-computation and aggregation signals <d-cite key="wang2019adaptive"></d-cite>.|
| Systems Heterogeneity | First check latency, memory demand, and device capacity, and then allocate different roles and models to agents accordingly to minimise inference costs and end-to-end execution time <d-cite key="yue2025masrouter,zhao2026scmas"></d-cite>. | Assign client workloads and select clients to participate in distributed training according to the available resources and system heterogeneity <d-cite key="lai2021oort,diao2021heterofl"></d-cite>.|
| Expensive Communication | Prune unnecessary information exchanges, redundant agents, or connections <d-cite key="zhang2025agentprune,wang2025agentdropout"></d-cite>.| Prune and quantize the gradients exchanged <d-cite key="lin2018compression,alistarh2017qsgd"></d-cite>, or prune redundant clients and connections <d-cite key="chen2018lag,wang2019matcha"></d-cite>.|
{: .do2ma-challenges .do2ma-efficiency-ideas}


## Trustworthiness
{: #trustworthiness-research}
Trustworthiness would be an important direction where such a connection would be pretty helpful. For example, in conventional distributed optimization, a series of threat models for robustness and privacy has been established:

- Malicious clients acting individually or in collusion can intentionally poison the dataset and uploaded model weights and gradients to corrupt the whole system, make the aggregated model suboptimal, or retrieve sensitive information from underlying clients.
- Malicious clients acting individually or in collusion can also deliberately and iteratively modify or construct uploaded model weights and gradients so that they can corrupt the system or retrieve sensitive information from underlying clients.
- A curious server can also try to retrieve sensitive information from underlying clients, either by itself or by working with some malicious clients.
  
Using the connection between these, we can easily construct similar threat models in multi-agent collaboration scenarios:

- Agents acting individually or in collusion can intentionally upload poisoned chains of thought or reasoning reflections to either prevent the final system from outputting a correct answer or retrieve task information and sensitive data from underlying agents.
- Malicious agents acting individually or in collusion can also deliberately revise correct reasoning traces and reflections so that the server agent or another agent may summarize them into a false memory.
- A curious server agent can, in some cases, work with other malicious agents to retrieve sensitive information. It can also try its best to retrieve underlying client agents' sensitive information by itself.

When we build such threat models, a natural idea would be to red-team these agents and let them self-evolve more robust, secure, and privacy-preserving reasoning or collaboration processes to defend against these threats.

Next, since a lot of defense methods have been established in the area of distributed optimization, we can similarly introduce these techniques into multi-agent collaboration, considering the similarity in patterns of data sharing and communication between multiple entities. The focus of tackling these challenges can be narrowed down to how we can transfer methods that operate on gradients and model parameters to methods that operate on plain text or reasoning trajectories, rather than the design of the entire system and its communication, making it easier to tackle these challenges.

| Challenges | Research Question Studied in Multi-Agent Collaboration | Ideas from Distributed Optimization |
|:-----------|:------------------------------------------------|:------------------------------------|
| Robustness | Use LLM agents to first detect malicious messages in order to restrict their propagation <d-cite key="amayuelas2024collaborationattack,wang2025gsafeguard"></d-cite>.| Design a Byzantine-robust aggregation algorithm to mitigate the impact of malicious agents <d-cite key="blanchard2017byzantine,yin2018byzantine"></d-cite>. |
| Safety and Security | Build guardrails to check if agent actions follow the principles and if safety constraints are satisfied <d-cite key="xiang2025guardagent,debenedetti2024agentdojo,wang2025gsafeguard"></d-cite>. | When the server receives the model updates, design an algorithm to validate these updates before aggregation <d-cite key="xie2019zeno,xie2020zenoplus"></d-cite>. |
| Privacy | For each agent, before uploading the traces, use agents to check whether the information to be shared contains any sensitive information and modify it accordingly <d-cite key="juneja2025magpie,li2025check,shao2024privacylens"></d-cite>. |Add random noise or some manipulated perturbation to the gradient to be shared with other entities <d-cite key="bonawitz2017secure,bell2020secure"></d-cite>. |
{: .do2ma-challenges .do2ma-trustworthiness-ideas}

From these examples, we can see that, when facing new challenges in multi-agent reasoning, instead of directly addressing these problems, we can first revisit previous high-level and methodological ideas in conventional distributed optimization. Next, we find the counterpart of each component in distributed optimization within multi-agent collaboration (e.g., mapping model updates to shared reasoning traces and mapping Byzantine attacks by malicious workers to Byzantine poisoning by malicious agents) and then adapt distributed optimization techniques to resolve challenges in multi-agent collaboration more clearly.

# Future Work

In this post, we propose an analogy between multi-agent reasoning and distributed optimization so that we can transfer well-established ideas and formulations from distributed optimization to the more challenging setting of multi-agent collaboration. Finally, we discuss potential future directions for multi-agent collaboration and how this connection can help us approach them.

**Pareto Frontiers in Multi-Agent Systems and Collaboration**. As discussed in the performance challenges, different agents may have different objectives. Moreover, each agent may need to balance multiple objectives and find a Pareto frontier representing trade-offs between accuracy, efficiency, and privacy. Conventional distributed optimization faces similar challenges. In the future, we need to explore how to adapt established Pareto optimization theory to the text space.

**Novel Models of Asynchrony**. FoH <d-cite key="yao2026federation"></d-cite> proposes a structure in which agents actively pull the harness from a GitHub repository when they need to solve new tasks, rather than having the server agent actively send out a new harness. However, extending this structure to peer-to-peer and asynchronous settings remains unexplored. Distributed optimization includes fully decentralized schemes without a participating server, allowing more device-centric communication. It also includes event-triggered schemes in which each device can decide when to “wake up” and interact with the central server. We should explore similar approaches in multi-agent collaboration.

**Autonomous Systems and Recursive Self-Improvement**. An important aspect of multi-agent systems and collaboration is recursive self-improvement, where agents iteratively update their memory and LLMs to become more capable. As the whole system operates autonomously, an important direction is how to enable multi-agent collaboration itself to evolve. In conventional distributed optimization, adaptive optimization and continual learning help systems adapt over time. How to combine these ideas with the new challenges of RSI is an important future direction.

**Better Agent Relations and a One-for-All System**. An important underexplored direction is how to clearly formalize relationships between agents using graph theory or other techniques. We can then use these relationships to improve the system and work toward a unified system that can solve different tasks together. In distributed optimization, researchers have explored how to construct graphs relating computational nodes, optimize these graphs, and use neural architecture search to identify relationships between nodes that provide the best performance.

**Theoretically Guaranteed Privacy**. Not all text-level reasoning necessarily contains sensitive information. Some trajectories contain structural or task-level information, such as what agents are doing or whether they are performing banking tasks, while actual bank identifiers can be hidden. Nevertheless, we still seek formal privacy guarantees, such as differential privacy. In conventional methods operating in parameter space, it is more straightforward to implement differential privacy or cryptographic methods such as homomorphic encryption and secure multiparty computation. At the text and chain-of-thought level, this is harder, and proving theoretical guarantees remains a challenging and important future direction.

**Industry-Level Products**. Today, there are many well-established products based on distributed optimization. Multi-agent collaboration still faces multiple challenges in deployment, including building infrastructure to run agents continuously, handling variations in device behavior at different times of the day or week, and addressing cold-start problems when new devices enter the network. We need to examine the steps taken to turn distributed optimization into products and apply those lessons to multi-agent collaboration.

**Data and Computation Markets**. In conventional distributed optimization, such as distributed training and federated learning, an interesting question is how to set prices and establish market standards for entities that contribute computational resources and data. Existing platforms for renting computational resources range from services offered by large companies, such as Google Cloud and AWS, to startups such as Together AI. In multi-agent collaboration, how to price agent computation, harness contributions, and task contributions, and establish rules for these markets, remains an open question.

# Concluding Remarks

In this post, we build a connection between distributed optimization and multi-agent collaboration. With mapping components including distributed , model updates, and aggregation mechanisms to agents, shared reasoning traces, and agent aggregation, we can adapt established ideas to help address challenges in performance, efficiency, and trustworthiness. The connection provides a systematic starting point for future work on agents where we can approach easier and cleaner.
